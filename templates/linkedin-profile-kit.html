How to use this template

This kit gives you plug-and-play formulas for the three parts of your LinkedIn that actually matter: your headline, your About section, and your profile setup. Fill in the formulas, copy to LinkedIn, done.

  • Three headline formulas — pick the one that fits your situation.
  • A paragraph-by-paragraph About section framework with strategic prompts.
  • Examples use two fictional people: Jordan (graduate looking for a first role in product management) and Sam (mid-career professional switching from operations to strategy). Delete after use.
  • Takes 30 minutes if you already know your story. Takes 60 if you need to think about it — that thinking is the point.

1. Headline

Your headline is the most-read line on your profile. It shows up in search results, connection requests, and comments. Most people default to their job title — a missed opportunity.

The rule

Your headline should answer: "What do you do, where, and why should I care?" in under 120 characters. Lead with what you do, not where you work.

Formula A — For graduates & job seekers

Formula
[Degree] | [Most impressive experience] | [Specific skill or interest]
Use this if you're a student or recent graduate. Lead with your degree only if it's relevant to the roles you want. Otherwise lead with experience.
Example — Jordan (graduate)

BSc Computer Science (First Class) | Product Intern @ Spotify | Interested in B2B SaaS

Formula B — For professionals with a clear role

Formula
[What you do] | [Where / who you do it for] | [One specific differentiator]
Use this if you have a defined role. The differentiator is what makes you memorable — a metric, a specialism, a niche.
Example — Sam (mid-career)

Operations → Strategy | Helping SaaS companies scale from 50 to 500 people | Ex-Deliveroo, Ex-Revolut

Formula C — For founders & freelancers

Formula
[What you build / sell] for [who] | [Proof point or company name]
Example

I design brand identities for early-stage startups | 40+ brands launched | Founder @ StudioNorth

Common mistakes

"Open to work" — use LinkedIn's "Open to Work" frame instead of wasting headline space.
"Passionate about..." — too vague to be useful. Replace with a specific skill or result.
Just your job title — "Marketing Manager" is generic. "Marketing Manager | B2B SaaS | Grew pipeline 3x at [Company]" is specific and memorable.

2. About section

The About section is 4 paragraphs, each with a specific purpose. Every sentence should either build credibility or make someone want to talk to you.

The rule

LinkedIn shows the first ~3 lines before "see more." Those 3 lines decide whether anyone reads the rest. Front-load your strongest sentence.

Paragraph 1 — The hook

What to write
One sentence that says who you are and what you do — with enough specificity that a recruiter knows instantly whether to keep reading.
Avoid opening with "I am a..." — it lacks impact. Lead with what you do, what you've achieved, or what you're known for. Include your degree or company only if they add credibility.
Your paragraph 1

[Write your opening sentence here. This is the most important sentence on your profile.]

Example — Jordan (graduate)

Final-year Computer Science student at the University of Leeds (First Class), with hands-on product experience at Spotify — where I shipped a feature used by 2M+ users in my first internship.

Example — Sam (mid-career)

I've spent 6 years in operations at high-growth startups (Deliveroo, Revolut) and I'm now moving into strategy — because I've seen what happens when companies scale without it.

Paragraph 2 — The evidence

What to write
2–3 sentences that prove you're good at what you do. Use specific projects, metrics, or outcomes — not adjectives.
Evidence beats adjectives. Instead of "strong communicator," write "presented risk scenarios to the executive team weekly." Instead of "results-driven," write "grew the pipeline from 200 to 600 leads in one quarter."
Your paragraph 2

[Write 2–3 sentences with specific evidence. What did you do? What was the result?]

Example — Jordan

At Spotify, I ran the discovery phase for a playlist collaboration feature — user research, wireframes, and the pitch to the product lead. The feature shipped 3 months later and is now used by 2M+ listeners monthly. Before that, I led the tech team for our university hackathon (300 participants, 12 sponsors).

Paragraph 3 — What you're interested in

What to write
1–2 sentences about what you care about professionally. This helps recruiters and connection requests understand what kind of conversations you want.
Be specific. "I'm interested in technology" means nothing. "I'm interested in how B2B SaaS companies find product-market fit in vertical markets" means something.
Your paragraph 3

[What are you genuinely interested in? What problems do you want to work on?]

Example — Sam

I'm interested in how high-growth companies make the transition from "move fast and break things" to "move fast and don't break the business." That's where operations meets strategy — and it's where I want to build my career.

Paragraph 4 — The close

What to write
One sentence that invites the right people to reach out. Be specific about what you want.
Avoid generic phrases like "open to opportunities" or "let's connect." Specify what kind of opportunities you want and why someone should reach out to you.
Your paragraph 4

[One sentence: what you're looking for and what kind of people you want to hear from.]

Example — Jordan

I'm looking for graduate product management roles at B2B SaaS companies (London preferred) — happy to connect with PMs, founders, or anyone hiring for product teams.

Example — Sam

Always happy to talk with people in strategy, ops, or leadership at scaling companies — especially if you've made the ops-to-strategy transition yourself.

4. Profile checklist

Your headline and About are 80% of the impact. The rest takes 10 minutes:

ElementWhat to doWhy it matters
Profile photo Professional headshot. Face fills 60%+ of the frame. Plain or blurred background. Smile. Profiles with photos get 21x more views. A bad photo is worse than no photo.
Banner image Simple branded image, a cityscape, or a photo of you at a relevant event. Not the default blue. The banner is the first thing people see. Default = you don't care about your profile.
Experience For each role: 2–3 bullet points. Start each with a verb. Include one metric if possible. Recruiters scan bullets, not paragraphs. Verb + result is the format.
Education Degree, university, grade (if good), relevant modules or societies. Include societies/clubs — they show leadership and initiative outside the classroom.
Featured Pin 1–3 things: a project, an article, a post that did well, or a portfolio link. The Featured section is prime real estate. Use it to show work, not just describe it.
Skills Add 10+ relevant skills. Pin the top 3 that match the roles you want. LinkedIn's search algorithm uses skills to match you to jobs and recruiters.
URL Customise to linkedin.com/in/yourname (Settings → Edit public profile URL). Looks professional on your CV. "linkedin.com/in/jordan-chen" > "linkedin.com/in/user-38291847"

5. Experience bullet formula

Each role on your profile should have 2–3 bullets. Use this formula:

Formula
[Action verb] + [what you did] + [result or impact]
Start every bullet with a strong verb: Led, Built, Shipped, Designed, Launched, Managed, Grew, Reduced, Presented, Automated. Avoid starting with "Responsible for" or "Helped with."
Weak example vs strong example

Weak: Responsible for helping the marketing team with campaigns and social media management across platforms

Strong: Ran paid social campaigns across Meta and Google Ads — grew lead pipeline from 200 to 580 MQLs in one quarter (190% increase)

Weak: Helped with a project that improved the onboarding process

Strong: Redesigned the customer onboarding flow — reduced time-to-first-value from 14 days to 3 days, improving 30-day retention by 22%

6. Posting strategy (optional but powerful)

You don't have to post on LinkedIn. But if you do, even once a month, it dramatically increases who sees your profile.

The easiest post formats

FormatWhat to writeExample hook
Lesson learned Something you learned at work or in a project. 3–5 short paragraphs. "The biggest mistake I made in my first internship..."
Contrarian take A professional opinion most people would disagree with. Back it up. "Unpopular opinion: your CV doesn't matter as much as you think."
Framework / tip Something useful. A template, a checklist, a process you use. "Here's the 5-step framework I use for every product decision..."
Milestone Started a new role, finished a project, hit a goal. Keep it humble. "Last week I shipped my first feature to production. Here's what I learned."
Consistency compounds

Post once every 2 weeks — 26 posts a year. Most professionals post nothing. Consistent, moderate output outperforms sporadic brilliance on LinkedIn.

What not to post

"Thrilled to announce..." — overused and reads as corporate boilerplate.
Motivational quotes — undermine professional credibility.
Engagement bait — "Like if you agree!" damages your credibility.
Long humblebrags — self-congratulatory narratives disguised as humility rarely land well.

7. Final review

Before you publish, check these:

  • Headline — under 120 characters, answers "what do you do and why should I care?"
  • About section — first 3 lines are strong enough to make someone click "see more"
  • No adjectives without evidence — delete "passionate," "driven," "motivated" unless followed by proof
  • Experience bullets — every bullet starts with a verb and includes a result
  • Profile photo — professional, face fills the frame, good lighting
  • Banner — not the default LinkedIn blue
  • Featured section — at least one pinned item
  • Custom URL — linkedin.com/in/yourname
  • Read it out loud — if it sounds like corporate jargon, rewrite it in your own voice
Remember

Your LinkedIn profile serves a different purpose to your CV. A CV lists what you've done. LinkedIn tells people who you are, what you care about, and whether they want to work with you. Write like a person, not a job description.
