Creative CV — Strategy Guide

For marketing, media, design, and communications roles

Creative industries hire differently. Recruiters at agencies, studios, and media companies want to see evidence of creative thinking, measurable impact, and a distinctive voice — all within a format that still passes ATS screening. This guide shows you how.

1. Why a Profile Summary Works Here

In finance and consulting, a profile summary is unnecessary — your credentials speak first. In creative roles, a two-to-three sentence profile summary is expected and valued. It tells the reader who you are, what you care about, and what kind of creative work you do.

Scribed Insight Your profile summary should read as a pitch, not a bio. Lead with your creative identity, then anchor it with one concrete proof point.

A strong profile summary answers three questions in under 40 words:

  1. What's your creative discipline? (e.g. brand strategy, content design, social media)
  2. What's your most impressive proof point? (a metric, an award, a notable client)
  3. What are you looking for? (graduate role, placement, internship)
Example: "Final-year Communications student with a focus on brand storytelling. My design blog reaches 5,000 monthly readers and I was shortlisted for the D&AD New Blood Pencil. Seeking a graduate role in creative strategy."

What to avoid

  • Vague statements: "I'm a passionate creative" — every applicant says this
  • Life stories: keep it to 2–3 sentences maximum
  • Subjective claims without evidence: "excellent communicator" means nothing without a metric

2. Describing Campaigns and Content with Metrics

Creative work often feels hard to quantify, but recruiters still want numbers. The key is matching the right metrics to the right type of work.

Metric cheat sheet by role type

  • Social media: impressions, engagement rate, follower growth, click-through rate
  • Content / editorial: monthly readers, page views, time on page, subscriber growth
  • Campaign / advertising: reach, conversions, brand lift, awards/shortlists
  • Design: project turnaround time, client satisfaction, iteration count, adoption rate
Bullet Formula [Action verb] + [what you created/managed] + [measurable result]. Example: "Designed a 6-part Instagram carousel series for a product launch, generating 14K impressions and a 6.2% engagement rate."

If you genuinely don't have metrics (e.g. university projects), describe scope and outcome instead:

"Led a 4-person team to develop a 60-second spec ad for Nike as part of a brief competition — selected as best in class by external judges."

3. Portfolio Links — Where and How

Your portfolio is your most important asset in a creative application. The CV gets you to the interview; the portfolio gets you the job.

Placement rules

  1. Contact header: Always include your portfolio URL directly in the contact line, next to email and phone. This is the primary placement.
  2. Projects section: If you have a dedicated Projects/Portfolio section, you can include it again with a brief description.
  3. Do not bury it at the bottom. If a recruiter can't find it in the first 3 seconds, it doesn't exist.
ATS Warning Write your portfolio URL as plain text (rileypark.design) rather than as a hyperlink label like "View Portfolio." ATS systems parse plain-text URLs more reliably than masked hyperlinks.

Portfolio URL formatting

  • Use a clean custom domain if possible: rileypark.design > rileypark.myportfolio.com
  • If using Behance or Dribbble, that's fine — just make sure the URL is clean
  • Never link to a Google Drive folder as your "portfolio"

4. Personality vs. Professionalism — Finding the Balance

Creative CVs can have more personality than a finance CV, but "more" doesn't mean "a lot." The CV itself should be clean and restrained. Save the full creative expression for your portfolio.

What's allowed

  • A thin accent rule under your name (subtle, not a rainbow gradient)
  • Slightly more generous whitespace and a larger name treatment
  • A profile summary written in your own voice (not corporate-speak)
  • Mentioning personal creative projects (a blog, a zine, a YouTube channel)

What's not allowed

  • Skill bars, pie charts, infographics, or icons as section markers
  • Two-column or sidebar layouts (ATS reads top-to-bottom, left-to-right)
  • Decorative fonts, bright colours, or large graphics
  • Replacing section headings with clever alternatives ("The Journey So Far" instead of "Experience")
The Rule If someone printed your CV in greyscale on a standard office printer, would it still look professional and be fully readable? If yes, you're within bounds.

5. Section Order for Creative Roles

This template follows the optimal order for entry-level creative applications:

  1. Name & Contact — including portfolio link prominently
  2. Profile Summary — 2–3 sentences; your creative identity and top proof point
  3. Experience — internships, freelance, part-time roles, in reverse chronological order
  4. Education — degree, relevant modules, grade if strong
  5. Projects / Portfolio — personal and university creative projects with outcomes
  6. Skills — tools, platforms, languages; grouped by category

Experience comes before education in creative fields because agencies care more about what you've made than where you studied. If you have no experience yet, swap the order.
